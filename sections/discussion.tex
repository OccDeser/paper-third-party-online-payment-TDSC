\section{Discussion}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In this paper, we reveal and formally verify the widely prevalent Order Tampering attack, and propose a user-side order authentication mechanism to fundamentally mitigate this class of attacks.
The core idea is to introduce a user authentication public key, denoted as $Pk_{UA}$, for each user–merchant pair and bind it to the merchant identifier during the initial payment.
It enables the user side to perform a secondary verification of the order origin and integrity.
While preserving the existing payment experience, the proposed mechanism introduces order authentication as an explicit user-side trust anchor and narrows the attack surface from order tampering over arbitrary insecure channels to the compromise of locally bound keys, and significantly increasing the attack difficulty.
Through formal verification, we show that under the assumption of insecure channels between \emph{MF}--\emph{MS} and \emph{MF}--\emph{PS}, the proposed model satisfies all the security properties defined in this work.

\paragraph{Advantages.}
From a protocol design perspective, our countermeasure exhibits three key advantages.
First, \textbf{minimal trust reduction}: the set of channels that must be trusted is reduced from all channels to only the human–computer interaction channel and the channel used to communicate with the \emph{PS-Server}.
Second, \textbf{backward compatibility}: our payment path closely resembles existing payment models, with only additional logic for signature verification and local key binding introduced at the payment side.
Third, \textbf{formally provable security}: our Tamarin model demonstrates that the proposed countermeasure effectively prevents order tampering attacks and satisfies all the security properties defined in this paper.

\paragraph{Limitations.} 
We honestly present the limitations and trade-offs of the proposed countermeasure.
(\rom{1}) \textbf{Security vulnerability during the initial payment}: 
during the first payment, no public key has yet been bound on the user side, and thus order tampering attacks cannot be prevented. 
As a result, the initial payment, similar to existing payment models, requires all communication channels to be secure.
(\rom{2}) \textbf{User interaction cost during the initial payment}: 
the initial binding requires presenting trust prompts to the user and obtaining explicit confirmation, which increases the user interaction overhead.
(\rom{3}) \textbf{Signature and storage overhead}: 
introducing additional signatures and local storage incurs computational and implementation complexity. 
However, no new cryptographic primitives are introduced, and the signature algorithms can be directly reused from those already deployed in existing payment systems. 
Moreover, modern servers and smartphones are sufficiently powerful to handle signing and verification operations, and the storage overhead is small.
Therefore, the overall cost is acceptable in practical deployments.

With respect to mitigating the vulnerabilities we identified, we recommend adopting a phased remediation strategy.
In the short term, merchants and payment service providers should strengthen the protection of communication channels.
On the Android platform, explicit Intents should be enforced to prevent the risk of implicit Intent hijacking.
In the medium to long term, the proposed countermeasure can be gradually rolled out to high-value or high-risk merchants before being incrementally extended to all payment scenarios.
