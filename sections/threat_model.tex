\section{Threat Model and Assumptions}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before introducing the formal models and security properties, 
we first clarify the threat model and the security assumptions made herein.

\subsection{Threat Model}

The threat model considered for our formal analysis and attack is defined as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The adversary can obtain legitimate payment parameters for arbitrary products from any merchant platform.
  \item The adversary follows the standard Dolev-Yao model and is able to fully control insecure communication channels, including eavesdropping, modifying, replaying, and injecting arbitrary messages.
\end{enumerate}

The assumed adversary model reflects realistic threats in real-world third-party online payment systems.
Merchant platforms are typically public, 
allowing an adversary to easily register a legitimate account and purchase arbitrary products to obtain payment parameters.
In third-party online payment ecosystems,
not all communication channels between different entities are secure, and the adversary can control those that are insecure.
We will elaborate on the security properties of all communication channels in Section~\ref{sec:security_assumptions}.

\subsection{Security Assumptions} \label{sec:security_assumptions}

\paragraph{User Assumptions}
In Section~\ref{sec:payment-ecosystem}, we describe that \emph{U} confirms the order information before authorizing the payment during the \emph{Payment} phase.
We assume that users can accurately determine whether the payment \emph{amount} and order \emph{description} shown on the payment interface are consistent with the products they selected on \emph{MF}.
In particular, we assume that \emph{U} will not ignore or misinterpret the payment \emph{amount} or order \emph{description}, and thus will not be misled into authorizing a payment.

Another important piece of information displayed on the payment interface is the payee account name.
However, as this name does not necessarily correspond to the merchant's brand name, \emph{U} cannot reliably determine if the displayed account belongs to the intended merchant.
For instance, when purchasing a video game on Steam (a leading global digital game distribution and social platform), the payee name shown is ``Valve Corporation'' (the parent company) rather than ``Steam''.
A user without relevant background knowledge may find it difficult to determine whether ``Valve Corporation'' is indeed the payee account associated with ``Steam''.
Therefore, we assume that users ignore the payee account name when authorizing a payment.

\paragraph{Payment System Assumptions}
The \emph{PS} comprises two components: the \emph{PS-App} and the \emph{PS-Server}. 
We assume both the \emph{PS-App} and the \emph{PS-Server} are trusted and cannot be compromised by an adversary. 
Furthermore, as they communicate via an internal secure channel, we assume the adversary cannot tamper with or forge messages exchanged between them. 
To simplify subsequent analysis, we abstract them as a single, unified \emph{PS} entity.

\paragraph{Channel Assumptions}
The developer manuals of various payment providers typically state that the \emph{MS} should be properly configured with security protocols such as HTTPS to ensure secure communication.
This implies that all channels where \emph{MS} participates should be secure.
We argue that this assumption is overly strict, as payment providers cannot fully guarantee the security of communications between users and merchants.
Therefore, we propose to relax the assumption of universally secure channels and analyze whether third-party payment systems can guarantee payment security even under partially insecure channels.
We consider the following communication channels to be potentially insecure:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\Rom{1}.] \textbf{\emph{MF} $\Longleftrightarrow$ \emph{MS}:}
  We assume that the communication channel between the \emph{MF} and \emph{MS} is insecure.
  We argue that this assumption is realistic for two reasons.
  First, the user's network environment, which is beyond the control of merchants and payment providers, may be insecure.
  Second, payment providers cannot guarantee that all merchants have correctly configured the required security protocols or that their configurations are free of vulnerabilities.
\item[\Rom{2}.] \textbf{\emph{MF} $\Longleftrightarrow$ \emph{PS}:}
  The communication between \emph{MF} and \emph{PS} occurs during the \emph{Prepayment} phase, where \emph{MF} invokes the \emph{PS-App} to transfer payment parameters.
  However, this process is not absolutely secure.
  We observe that in many implementations, this interaction is vulnerable to hijacking attacks.
  In fact, we have identified real-world attacks exploiting this vulnerability, which will be discussed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:real_world}.
  Therefore, we assume that this channel is insecure.
\end{enumerate}
