\section{Security Properties} \label{sec:security_properties}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In this section, we formally define the security properties of third-party online payment protocols.
The core security goal of such protocols is to ensure that the user, the merchant, and the payment service provider reach a consistent and unambiguous view of a transaction.
In particular, the protocol must guarantee that (a) all parties agree on the payment order being processed, (b) each payment is explicitly authorized by the user, and (c) the reported payment result faithfully reflects the actual fund transfer.

All properties considered in this section are instances of \emph{injective agreement}~\cite{Lowe1997AuthenticationHierarchy}.
Injective agreement strengthens standard agreement by additionally requiring a one-to-one correspondence between protocol runs: 
each successful local completion must correspond to a unique execution of the peer, thereby ruling out replay, duplication, or reuse of protocol messages.
Formally, two types of atomic facts are necessary to define injective agreement:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\mathsf{Running}(A,B,x)$: 
    records that party $A$ is interacting with $B$ in a concrete execution of the protocol and that the interaction involves data $x$ (an objective execution fact);
  \item $\mathsf{Commit}(A,B,x)$:
    denotes that party $A$ subjectively believes that it has reached agreement with $B$ on data $x$ (e.g., after receiving and accepting a message).
\end{enumerate}
This notion is particularly well-suited for online payment scenarios, where both consistency and uniqueness of transactions are essential.
We structure the security properties according to the logical progression of a payment protocol: 
agreement on the \emph{order}, agreement on \emph{user authorization}, and agreement on the \emph{result}.


\subsection{Injective Agreement on Order}

Injective agreement on the order ensures that all involved parties process the same payment order and that each order is handled at most once.
Violations of this property indicate that an adversary may have tampered with the order information or caused inconsistent views among the participants.
Since this property involves three principals, we separately consider the agreements between \emph{PS} and \emph{MS}, and between \emph{U} and \emph{MS}.

\textbf{IA-O1~(\emph{PS} $\leftarrow$ \emph{MS}):} 
This lemma captures injective agreement on the order from the perspective of \emph{PS}.
If \emph{PS} accepts a payment request for an order allegedly issued by \emph{MS}, then \emph{MS} must have previously initiated a corresponding request for that order.
Moreover, no other payment request for the same order can be accepted by \emph{PS}, ensuring uniqueness.
The property is formalized as follows:
\begin{tightflalign}
  \TAll \; \text{\Tterm{ps}} & \text{\Tterm{ ms order \#i.}} \; \text{\TFact{PSCommit(ps, ms, order)@\#i}} &\\
  \implies & \TEx \; \text{\TLemma{\#j.}} \; \text{\TFact{MSRunning(ms, ps, order)@\#j}} \; \land\\
  \lnot \; &(\;\TEx \; \text{\TLemma{ps2 ms2 \#i2.}} \; \text{\Tterm{\#i2}} \neq \text{\Tterm{\#i}} \; \land\\
  & \hspace{2em} \text{\TFact{PSCommit(ps2, ms2, order)@\#i2}}) 
\end{tightflalign}
Here, the \emph{Commit} and \emph{Running} facts follow the standard interpretation introduced in~\cite{Lowe1997AuthenticationHierarchy}.
The fact \TFact{PSCommit(ps, ms, order)} denotes that \emph{PS} has accepted a payment request for \Tterm{order}, 
while \TFact{MSRunning(ms, ps, order)} records that \emph{MS} has actually issued such a request.
The term \Tterm{order} denotes all security-critical order information, including the merchant's order identifier, the payment amount, and the product description.

\textbf{IA-O2~(\emph{U} $\leftarrow$ \emph{MS}):} 
From the user’s perspective, injective agreement on the order ensures that the user and the merchant reach agreement on order information during the payment process, and that each order is processed exactly once.
The formalization is as follows:
\begin{tightflalign}
  \TAll \; \text{\Tterm{u }} & \text{\Tterm{ms goods \#i.}} \; \text{\TFact{UMCommit(u, ms, goods)@\#i}} &\\
  \implies & \TEx \; \text{\TLemma{\#j.}} \; \text{\TFact{MSRunning(ms, u, goods)@\#j}} \; \land\\
  \lnot \; &(\;\TEx \; \text{\TLemma{u2 ms2 \#i2.}} \; \text{\Tterm{\#i2}} \neq \text{\Tterm{\#i}} \; \land\\
  & \hspace{2em} \text{\TFact{UMCommit(u2, ms2, goods)@\#i2}}) 
\end{tightflalign}
Unlike the order information considered in IA-O1, IA-O2 only requires agreement between the user and the merchant on the purchased goods, since the user is primarily concerned with what is being bought.


\subsection{Injective Agreement on User Authorization}

Injective agreement on user authorization ensures that each successful payment is backed by a unique and explicit authorization from the user.
This property prevents both unauthorized payments and reuse of a single authorization to complete multiple transactions.

\textbf{IA-UA~(\emph{PS} $\leftarrow$ \emph{U}):}
Specifically, if \emph{PS} completes a payment based on a password hash, then the corresponding password must have been input by \emph{U}, and each password input can authorize at most one payment:
\begin{tightflalign}
  \TAll \; \text{\Tterm{u }} & \text{\Tterm{p s \#i.}} \; \text{\TFact{PaymentComplete(u, h(p), s)@\#i}} &\\
  \implies & \TEx \; \text{\TLemma{\#j.}} \; \text{\TFact{UserInputPwd(u, p, s)@\#j}} \; \land\\
  \lnot \; &(\;\TEx \; \text{\TLemma{u2 p2 \#i2.}} \; \text{\Tterm{\#i2}} \neq \text{\Tterm{\#i}} \; \land\\
  & \hspace{2em} \text{\TFact{PaymentComplete(u2, h(p2), s)@\#i2}}) 
\end{tightflalign}
In this lemma, the fact \TFact{UserInputPwd(u, p, s)} represents that the user \Tterm{u} inputs the password \Tterm{p} in a session \Tterm{s} to authorize a payment.
The fact \TFact{PaymentComplete(u, h(p), s)} denotes that \emph{PS} has received the hash value \Tterm{h(p)} of the password \Tterm{p} sent by \Tterm{u}, and has completed the payment.


\subsection{Injective Agreement on Result}

Finally, injective agreement on the payment result guarantees that successful payment notifications cannot be forged, replayed, or duplicated.
A violation of this property would allow an adversary to falsely convince the user or the merchant that a payment has been completed, even though no actual fund transfer has occurred.
We again consider the perspectives of \emph{MS} and \emph{U} separately.

\textbf{IA-R1~(\emph{MS} $\leftarrow$ \emph{PS}):}
From the merchant's view, if \emph{MS} receives a successful payment notification for an order, then this notification must have been generated by \emph{PS}.
Furthermore, \emph{MS} must not receive multiple successful notifications for the same order.
This requirement is captured by the following lemma:
\begin{tightflalign}
  \TAll \; \text{\Tterm{ms}} & \text{\Tterm{ ps order \#i.}} \; \text{\TFact{MSCommit(ms, ps, order)@\#i}} &\\
  \implies & \TEx \; \text{\TLemma{\#j.}} \; \text{\TFact{PSRunning(ps, ms, order)@\#j}} \; \land\\
  \lnot \; &(\;\TEx \; \text{\TLemma{ms2 ps2 \#i2.}} \; \text{\Tterm{\#i2}} \neq \text{\Tterm{\#i}} \; \land\\
  & \hspace{2em} \text{\TFact{MSCommit(ms2, ps2, order)@\#i2}}) 
\end{tightflalign}

\textbf{IA-R2~(\emph{U} $\leftarrow$ \emph{PS}):}
Similarly, from the user’s perspective, if \emph{U} observes a successful payment indication in the payment application, then \emph{PS} must have sent a corresponding notification.
Each order can lead to at most one such notification.
Formally:
\begin{tightflalign}
  \TAll \; \text{\Tterm{u }} & \text{\Tterm{ps goods \#i.}} \; \text{\TFact{UPCommit(u, ps, goods)@\#i}} &\\
  \implies & \TEx \; \text{\TLemma{\#j.}} \; \text{\TFact{PSRunning(ps, u, goods)@\#j}} \; \land\\
  \lnot \; &(\;\TEx \; \text{\TLemma{u2 ps2 \#i2.}} \; \text{\Tterm{\#i2}} \neq \text{\Tterm{\#i}} \; \land\\
  & \hspace{2em} \text{\TFact{UPCommit(u2, ps2, goods)@\#i2}}) 
\end{tightflalign}
