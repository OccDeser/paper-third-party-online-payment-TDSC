\section{Introduction}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Third-party payment systems have become a core infrastructure of modern e-commerce and mobile payments.
Particularly, third-party payment services in China have evolved into a foundational component of the digital economy, with both market scale and user penetration continuing to grow.
According to the latest industry report by iResearch~\cite{Iresearch2025ChinaThirdPartyPayment}, 
the transaction volume of China's personal third-party payment market has reached 67 trillion Chinese yuan (approximately 9.6 trillion US dollars) in 2025, 
with online payments accounting for more than $50\%$ of the total.
Among all third-party payment platforms, 
Alipay~\cite{alipay2026official} and WeChat Pay~\cite{wechatpay2026official} are the two dominant payment service providers in China, consistently occupying the top tier of the third-party payment market.

A typical third-party payment ecosystem comprises the User~(\emph{U}), the Merchant Frontend~(\emph{MF}), the Merchant backend Server~(\emph{MS}), and the Payment System~(\emph{PS}).
This ecosystem supports multiple payment scenarios, including in-app payments, mobile web payments, and QR-code-based payments.
% and spans multiple scenarios such as in-app payments, mobile web payments, and QR-code-based payments.
Within this ecosystem, payment service providers can ensure the security of communications within their own payment systems, 
such as the communication between the payment application~(\emph{PS-App}) and the payment server~(\emph{PS-Server}).
Additionally, they can protect the channel security between \emph{MS} and \emph{PS-Server} by restricting communication to HTTPS.
% However, the security of communications between external entities cannot be guaranteed by payment service providers.
However, payment service providers cannot guarantee the security of communications between external entities.

Unfortunately, numerous empirical studies have shown that insecure or misconfigured channels have led to various categories of security vulnerabilities in third-party payment workflows.
% Prior work has demonstrated that untrusted channels may give rise to at least the following three types of attacks, which are often intertwined:
Prior research has identifyed at least three types of attacks associated with untrusted channels:
(1) \emph{Order Injection and Tampering Over Insecure Channels}: 
Attackers can replace or replay order, causing users to unknowingly pay for incorrect orders~\cite{Sun2014DetectingECommerce, Yang2017FlawedImplementations};
(2) \emph{Misuse of Signatures and Parameter Structures}: 
Attackers can alter payment parameters without invalidating the signature by exploiting flawed signature designs~\cite{Lou2021PaymentVulnerabilities};
(3) \emph{Payment Result Forgery and UI Confusion}: 
Attackers can inject forged ``payment success'' messages or leverage UI overlay attacks, H5 redirection, and script injection to mislead merchants or users about the transaction status, enabling free purchases or misdirected payments~\cite{Ulqinaku2019ScanAndPayAndroid, Yang2017FlawedImplementations}.
These studies reveal a critical issue: the cryptographic mechanisms employed by current third-party online payment protocols are insufficient to withstand threats arising from insecure external channels.
From the perspective of payment service providers, \emph{adopting weaker security assumptions for external channels better reflects the relities of modern payment environments}.
This observation \emph{motivates us to analyze the security of third-party online payment protocols under weakened external channel security assumptions}.

In this paper, we establish comprehensive symbolic models for third-party online payment protocols.
By thoroughly examining the developer documentation of Alipay\cite{Alipay2025OpenPlatform} and WeChat Pay\cite{WeChat2025APIV3}, we abstract six representative payment protocol models across three core payment scenarios.
Our model faithfully reflects the design details of real-world protocols and covers the complete payment workflow from order placement to payment completion.
Based on the analyzed protocols, we abstract high-level design models for third-party payment protocols and investigate the common security issues at this level.
In addition, to capture capabilities of the adversary in the real world, we explicitly model the details of the adversary's behavior.
Particularly, we grant the adversary the ability to legitimately access merchant platforms to create orders and obtain payment parameters.

We formally define a set of security properties for third-party online payment protocols from three dimensions: order, authorization, and result.
Based on these properties, we conduct formal verification of the six real-world payment models under weakened channel security assumptions using the Tamarin Prover~\cite{Meier2013TAMARINProver}.
Our analysis reveals a pervasive yet underexplored design anti-pattern: mainstream high-level payment models generally lack a mechanism that binds user payment authorization to the intended order.
Consequently, once the communication channel between the user and the merchant is compromised, an adversary can induce the user to authorize an arbitrary order.

Inspired by our formal analysis results, we identify a new variant of the Order Tampering Attack.
We observe that many merchant applications on Android incorrectly use implicit Intents to interact with payment applications.
This misuse exposes the transmission of payment parameters to interception by malicious applications.
We develop proof-of-concept attack applications that exploit this vulnerability and successfully perform Order Tampering Attacks in real-world payment environments.
To assess the severity of this issue, we conduct a measurement study covering 461 popular applications across multiple domains, including online shopping, online education, entertainment, and gaming.
Our results show that more than $25\%$ of the sampled applications are vulnerable.
These applications collectively account for over 200 billion downloads, potentially affecting billions of users.

To address these threats, we propose a deployable and backward-compatible payment model which incorporates a user-side order authentication mechanism to establish a binding between user's authorization and the intended order.
Under the weakened channel assumptions, we formally verify this model and show that it satisfies all the defined security properties.
As a result, it prevents order tampering at the protocol design level while maintaining practical deployability.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Formal modeling and analysis.}
    We develop a unified symbolic modeling framework for third-party online payment protocols and apply it to rigorously analyze representative real-world protocols under realistic adversary and channel assumptions.
  \item \textbf{Discovery of a new attack.}
    We discover a new variant of the Order Tampering Attack that arises from a fundamental design flaw in high-level payment models and our measurement study demonstrates that it potentially affects over 25\% of real-world applications.
  \item \textbf{Design of a practical mitigation.}
    We propose a deployable and backward-compatible user-side authentication mechanism, and formally prove that it eliminates order tampering at the protocol design level while preserving real-world applicability.
\end{enumerate}
